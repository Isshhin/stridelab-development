# Session H | Coaching frameworks and competing physiological models

4 November 2026 • Research branch baseline `50b01a314c1ac0e3f45564c4df6cfcae2634c80d` • Phase 1 evidence review for Session G

**First experiment:** establish the repeatability of speed at a comparable easy HR under Session D's existing protocol. The athlete can supply a familiar, comfortably sustainable HR range. The experiment does not depend on accepting an age formula. Only after reliability is known should we ask whether this measure adds information to perceived effort and recent performance.

## Scope and evidence

Read the [program](2026-11-04-engine2x-parallel-research-program.md), [focused agenda](2026-11-04-focused-engine2-evidence-and-experiment-agenda.md), [Batch 8F](batch-8f-original-lab-admission-and-field-outcome-protocol-2026-11-03.md), [frequency and score addendum](2026-11-04-frequency-progression-and-score-limitations.md) and [session allocation analysis](runna-garmin-session-allocation-and-load-observations.md). The comparisons below concern documented research assumptions and prototypes. Current StrideLab can represent planned exposure and reviewed changes. The research record lacks a demonstration that a particular physiological policy improves an individual runner's outcomes.

This is a targeted narrative review. Searches paired originating authors and methods with independent tests, opposing interventions, individual agreement and null outcomes. Linked studies are SCIENCE except where identified as author practice or VENDOR. Source access is conservatively graded as abstract or indexed excerpt unless a specific full-text section is identified. No systematic screening, full-text risk-of-bias assessment, new app observations, athlete records or synthetic runs. Sample details given below are those verified in accessible material. Omitted demographic details are unverified. Same-author follow-up and reviews of the original studies are not independent replications.

## Claim register

Grades are for the precise component shown. **S** = SUPPORTED_CANDIDATE, **P** = PLAUSIBLE_NEEDS_VALIDATION, **C** = CONTESTED, **W** = WEAK_OR_UNSUPPORTED, **N** = NOT_SUITABLE_FOR_ENGINE_RULE. A supported measurement or mechanism only licenses the inference stated in its cell. Outcome grades distinguish the observed endpoint from a proposed coaching benefit.

## What supports or challenges each framework

**1. MAF testing versus MAF calibration.** The author's method combines adjusted 180-minus-age, easy training and repeat field testing. The original Høeg/Maffetone report is especially important: investigators first assigned HR using clinical/gait assessment and then derived the formula. In the experienced-runner cohort 170/223 improved their 5K. This is not an external test of formula-assigned HR. The abstract also gives incompatible second-group denominators in Methods and Results. Its injury estimate should not calibrate an engine. Achten and Jeukendrup measured Fatmax experimentally in trained men. An independent lipid-oxidation study found predicted zones could miss measured maxima, but did not directly test adjusted MAF. Conversely, standardized submaximal running measures have independent monitoring support, while Haaf et al. found HR alone inadequate during intensified training. These findings challenge specificity, not the feasibility of repeating a test. [Original derivation](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.wem.2015.03.015) · [Author's framework](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32300310/) · [Fatmax](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14598198/) · [Prediction discrepancy](https://www.nmcd-journal.com/article/S0939-4753%2823%2900283-1/abstract) · [Running monitoring review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9940993/) · [Overload limitation](https://doi.org/10.1080/17461391.2019.1571112)

**2. Distribution and threshold practice.** Seiler/Kjerland described relatively little middle-zone work in trained junior cross-country skiers. The denominator matters: warm-up, recoveries and HR lag mean that a hard session contains easy minutes. Both polarized and pyramidal plans emphasize easy work; the latter has more middle than high work. Stöggl/Sperlich's nine-week mixed-endurance intervention favored polarization on several physiological endpoints. Against a blanket preference, the individual-participant synthesis included 348 athletes across 13 studies (296 male, 52 female). Polarized and pyramidal interventions did not differ overall on VO₂max or time trial when classified by HR-zone time. Performance-level subgroup findings generate a stratification hypothesis. In recreational runners a focused middle-zone program and a polarized program both improved 2-km performance without significant between-group superiority (full-text Discussion). Bakken/Casado's elite practice combines controlled threshold intervals **with high easy volume**. It is a different intervention from making most training moderately hard. Sampling lactate to control reps and splitting threshold work over two daily sessions require separate tests. No independent evidence located here establishes the superiority of the split at equal dose in recreational runners. [Seiler/Kjerland](https://paulogentil.com/pdf/Quantifying%20training%20intensity%20distribution%20in%20elite%20endurance%20athletes%20-%20is%20there%20evidence%20for%20an%20optimal%20distribution.pdf) · [Intervention](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24550842/) · [Individual-participant synthesis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39888556/) · [Focused comparison](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7739641/) · [Threshold framework, author practice/review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36900796/)

**3. Metabolic profiling versus lactate transport.** San Millán/Brooks compared lactate and indirect-calorimetry responses during graded exercise across professional endurance athletes, moderately active participants and people with metabolic syndrome. A between-group difference in a lab profile does not identify which training produced it. Brooks and colleagues' isotope work in trained and untrained men separates direct oxidation from indirect pathways. Independently, Gertz et al. measured increased myocardial lactate oxidation during moderate exercise in nine healthy trained men. This directly supports lactate as fuel but concerns one tissue and acute exercise. Faude et al. document multiple threshold definitions with different criteria. Protocol, sport and measurement method determine what a boundary means. Neither tracer work nor a group profile validates a universal 2 mmol/L target, equivalence to a five-zone watch label or a training dose for free-living runners. Preserve LT1, VT1 and Fatmax as separately named references. [Profile](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28623613/) · [Tracer study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23788576/) · [Independent myocardial experiment](https://www.jci.org/articles/view/113822) · [Threshold methods review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19453206/)

**4. Autonomic versus performance guidance.** Buchheit's framework calls for context, protocol and signal-specific interpretation. Kiviniemi tested daily HRV-guided endurance training against predefined training. Nuuttila's recreational-runner overload study found divergent subgroup responses, while subjective markers followed increased load more systematically than several HR measures. Manresa-Rocamora's meta-analysis did not establish a reliable between-group endurance-performance advantage. Ranieri et al. directly compared HR, race-pace and HRV prescription (28 recreational runners completing six weeks, groups of 9/9/10). Race-pace guidance improved time-trial performance within group. Small arms and differing endpoints limit inference about who benefits; a within-group change cannot settle superiority. Neither race-pace prescription nor post-hoc responder classification tests a live algorithm using serial performance. That comparison remains open. [Buchheit, framework](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24578692/) · [Kiviniemi, trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17849143/) · [Nuuttila, overload study](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsc.12115) · [Meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34639599/) · [Direct comparison](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000003671)

**5. Perceived versus external load.** Foster compared whole-session RPE with HR-based methods during steady, interval and team-sport exercise. Impellizzeri et al. independently found within-player relationships with HR-derived training loads in 19 young soccer players. Both are internal comparators. Correlation does not establish agreement, equivalence to distance, or prediction of future performance. Ten hard uphill minutes and a longer easy run can receive equal arbitrary-unit totals while exposing tissues and energy systems differently. Prompt timing, scale familiarity, missing responses and exercise type affect interpretation. Preserve the rating, actual duration and external structure. [Foster](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11708692/) · [Independent comparison](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15179175/) · [Measurement limitations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5673663/)

**6. Taper, reduced exposure and re-entry.** In Mujika's 18 elite swimmers a modeled decline in negative training influence accompanied taper improvement, but improvement during the third taper was not significant. A fitted model does not directly measure fatigue. Independently, Shepley compared three seven-day tapers in nine highly trained runners, supporting a role for retaining intensity while reducing volume in a planned precompetition taper. Hickson's reduced-frequency work shows aerobic power can persist with retained training. Chen found losses after two weeks of cessation in male endurance runners. These differ in exposure, endpoint and baseline. None assigns a safe re-entry dose after illness, injury or an unknown gap. Ascertain the reason before using training history. [Mujika primary study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8775162/) · [Taper trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1559951/) · [Reduced frequency](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7219129/) · [Cessation](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33517866/) · [Mujika/Padilla review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10966148/)

**7. Durability and environmental confounding.** Jones proposes that retaining physiological determinants under fatigue matters independently of fresh capacity. In 82,303 recreational marathoners, pace/HR-derived decoupling related to race performance. Pacing choices and using the same race to derive predictor and outcome constrain causal interpretation. Stevenson/Maunder measured a shift in VT1-related parameters after two hours of cycling in 14 trained cyclists/triathletes (one woman). This independent lab evidence supports changing physiology, without validating a running-watch surrogate. A fast-finish long run, a hot second half and inadequate fuel can all change the field statistic. A stable HR trace cannot rule out deteriorating economy. [Jones framework](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37606604/) · [Marathon field study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35511416/) · [Independent preload study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37495864/) · [Methodological limitations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12576026/)

**8. Heat strain.** Che Muhamed et al. varied humidity at 31°C during one-hour runs in 11 trained men and observed higher strain and reduced subsequent exercise capacity at high humidity. Racinais et al. studied male and female world-championship marathoners and race walkers in Doha. Among measured athletes, peak core temperature did not distinguish finishers from nonfinishers or those with a medical event from others. A lab effect of humidity and a null field discrimination result answer different questions. Neither a weather station nor a watch measures the runner's complete exposure or heat tolerance. Match route, time, wind, shade and acclimation as feasible. [Humidity experiment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28349085/) · [Doha field study](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2021-104569) · [Racinais consensus](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.12467)

**9. Concurrent training.** Hickson's high-frequency ten-week concurrent protocol found attenuation of strength development under that workload. Schumann et al.'s updated meta-analysis found no overall decrement in maximal strength or hypertrophy from adding aerobic training, while explosive strength gains were more vulnerable, especially in same-session comparisons. A subgroup divided by spacing cannot identify an optimal individual interval. Acute soreness, next-session quality and chronic hypertrophy are distinct endpoints. The original high-dose combination is weak evidence against an upper-body session paired with an easy run. [Hickson primary study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7193134/) · [Independent synthesis and endpoint-specific nulls](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8891239/)

**10. Competing load and injury models.** Hulin/Gabbett linked acute/chronic ratios with injury in elite rugby league. Impellizzeri et al. identify denominator instability, ambiguity in what a ratio represents and problems translating association to prevention. In a ten-month cluster trial of 482 elite youth footballers across 34 teams of both sexes, ACWR-guided management did not reduce reported health problems (RR 1.01, 95% CI 0.91–1.12). In running, Buist's 532 novices had similar injury incidence under two progression programs. Frandsen's 5,205 runners showed an association with a single run exceeding recent longest distance, distinct from weekly change. Different sports, outcomes and windows explain some disagreement. The running cohort did not test a warning or a preventive cutoff. [Rugby cohort](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26511006/) · [Methods critique](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32502973/) · [Randomized policy test](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33036995/) · [Novice trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17940147/) · [Session-distance cohort](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2024-109380)

## Experiments and falsifiers for the receiving sessions

All proposals below are HYPOTHESIS. The grades refer to the proposed use, not the author's entire framework. Use only purpose-admitted data. Freeze endpoints, exclusion rules, error tolerance and a meaningful effect before looking at evaluation outcomes. Estimate error within athletes, validate forward in time, and hold athletes out when evaluating transfer. Report exclusions, missingness, uncertainty, subgroup coverage and how often the model abstains. An underpowered null leaves the question open.

**Priority for synthesis.** First, F admits records for D's repeatability study. Next, B/E compare the incremental value of HRV and RPE on the same eligible sessions. C can evaluate retained exposure separately from interval length once interruption reasons exist. Distribution efficacy, lactate-guided dosing, durability-driven prescription and injury-prevention interventions require different study designs and remain later questions. No numerical parity or athlete-benefit estimate follows from this review.

**Decision under disagreement.** Record which independent observations agree, which conflict and which are absent. A hot, hilly run with slower pace at the same HR is ineligible evidence for a fitness downgrade. Repeated poor quality-session performance and elevated RPE remain worth reviewing even when HRV looks ordinary. If changing the zone definition, exposure window or denominator reverses the conclusion, show the ambiguity and withhold the proposed change. These are candidate review behaviors for G; validating the measurement comes before validating the decision.
