## What decision is missing?

A runner plans four eight-minute controlled threshold bouts. If a meeting starts early, three intact bouts may preserve the intended sustained effort. If every bout starts comfortably but becomes too demanding before eight minutes, shorter bouts are a different option. If even the opening pace is too hard, changing the clock leaves the problem in place. If the fourth bout was already missed yesterday, today's question is whether the next session still fits. The arithmetic alone cannot select among these decisions. Example doses illustrate the distinction only.

Reuse its revision boundary. The gap is evidence for choosing one feasible edit over another, conditional on the athlete's reason and the purpose of the workout. ## What human studies and coaching frameworks support

Targeted review, not systematic. Access depth is stated below. Sex, sample size and exposure are reported where verified. Pooled studies vary in those fields. No cited study randomized symptomatic runners across this nine-action menu.

| Evidence and access | Finding or coaching interpretation | Contradiction or translation limit |
|---|---|---|
| S1. [Seiler and Sjursen](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15387806/), SCIENCE, abstract. Twelve well-trained runners, nine men and three women, four self-paced sessions over four weeks. | With equal total work and equal work:rest ratios, one-minute bouts elicited lower peak oxygen uptake than two-, four- and six-minute bouts. Session formats had similar peak perceived exertion. Seiler's interval-design approach makes uninterrupted bout length a relevant variable. | Acute high-intensity experiment. Similar perceived effort did not mean identical physiology. Neither these data nor a coach's preferred interval length establish an optimal threshold adjustment for a fatigued novice. |
| S2. [Bosquet, Montpetit, Arvisais and Mujika](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17762369/), SCIENCE, abstract and indexed summaries. Meta-analysis of 27 competitive-athlete taper studies, competition or field performance. | Planned precompetition unloading can preserve intensity while reducing volume. Mujika's taper framework gives a reason to separate those levers when intensity is the specific target. | A planned taper with a known event is materially different from an unexpectedly difficult session. No taper percentage or universal priority transfers to illness, heat or poor execution. |
| S3. [Manresa-Rocamora et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34639599/), SCIENCE, pooled-result extract. Controlled endurance training interventions. | HRV-guided prescription had a small favorable performance estimate with uncertainty spanning no difference (SMD 0.20, 95% CI −0.09 to 0.48). | Neither automatic cancellation after low HRV nor ignoring persistent corroborated signals follows. The comparator was a training program, not today's individual decision. |
| S4. [Che Muhamed et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28349085/), SCIENCE, prior focused-agenda abstract review. Eleven trained men, controlled runs at 31°C across humidity conditions. | Higher humidity increased strain and impaired subsequent performance in these conditions. Relative effort and external speed can diverge. | No individualized pace correction or evidence that completing the original duration in the heat is beneficial. |
| S5. [Menges et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13243379/), SCIENCE, full-text discussion extract. Running versus cycling training interventions with heterogeneous populations and tests. | Pooled running performance difference was near zero with wide uncertainty. General aerobic work may transfer. | A null difference does not demonstrate equivalence. Race-specific running exposure, mechanics and terrain practice cannot be credited by converting cycle minutes to kilometres. |
| S6. [Jin et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42683395/), SCIENCE, indexed review summary, competitive and elite endurance athletes. [Van Hooren and Peake](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-018-0916-2), narrative review excerpt. | Limited comparative support for recovery modalities over passive recovery. A cooldown study also answers a different question from prescribing an easy session the next day. | Comfort or preference can favor easy movement without a demonstrated next-day performance advantage. |
| S7. [IOC acute respiratory illness consensus](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/56/19/1066), SCIENCE, return-to-sport section and exercise-challenge guidance. Clinical consensus for athletes after respiratory infection. | Return depends on severity and response. Concerning exertional symptoms such as chest discomfort or unusual breathlessness warrant stopping and medical assessment. | Some mild presentations permit graded return. Consensus does not validate a consumer app diagnosing illness, clearing return, or imposing a fixed rest duration. |

## Nine candidate decisions

Declare the session's primary purpose before choosing an edit: e.g., easy aerobic time, uninterrupted long-run exposure, controlled threshold minutes, race-specific pace rehearsal, or fast repetitions with good mechanics. Record which features must survive and which can change. A secondary purpose can be sacrificed explicitly. Confidence below is in selecting the action in the stated context, distinct from confidence that a calendar conflict exists. **P** means preview the session and all downstream changes and require explicit acceptance before saving. **K** means no calendar write or approval. **S** means stopping requires no approval, but a saved revision requires P.

| Action | Training intent, athlete state and constraint | Modification and remaining week | Expected tradeoff | Applicability / confidence / approval |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| KEEP | Planned effort remains feasible, athlete reports normal response, only adverse input is isolated or unreliable. No safety concern or hard constraint. | Retain session and week. Invite an in-session reassessment if observations conflict. | Avoids unnecessary edits but can miss early fatigue. | PARTIAL / MODERATE for abstaining from a sensor-only edit / K |
| REDUCE_VOLUME | Easy aerobic exposure is useful, available time is shorter, and ordinary easy effort remains comfortable. | Shorten continuous work, retain preparation and easy effort. Leave removed minutes off the calendar. | Less time on feet. If uninterrupted long duration was primary, flag partial intent. | DIRECT for time feasibility / MODERATE for preserving an easy opportunity / P |
| REDUCE_INTENSITY | Duration remains feasible, but intended internal effort is exceeded consistently at planned speed, corroborated by the athlete. | Ease external speed and reassess. If the physiological target itself must fall, label the lost quality stimulus and review the next key session. | Slower speed can preserve easy effort but sacrifices pace-specific practice. If strain persists, ending early remains available. | PARTIAL / MODERATE for direction, LOW for magnitude / P |
| SHORTEN_INTERVALS | Athlete can control the intended effort early in each bout but repeatedly loses control late. Shorter continuous exposure still serves part of the purpose. | Shorten remaining work bouts. Specify recovery, cap effort, avoid compensating with faster running or extra reps. Retain actual bout lengths. | More fragmented work can lose sustained exposure and change oxygen kinetics. | PARTIAL / LOW / P |
| REDUCE_REPS | Completed bouts retain intended duration and quality. Remaining time or cumulative tolerability limits the set. | Omit whole remaining reps. Retain recovery between completed bouts and do not replay the omitted tail. | Preserves individual-bout specificity, reduces total quality minutes. | PARTIAL / LOW for choosing this over shorter bouts / P |
| SUBSTITUTE | Original equipment, route or modality unavailable. Athlete can do the alternative, and its feasible purpose is explicit. | Prefer comparable same-mode work when practical. Offer familiar cycling for general aerobic exposure if acceptable. Record modality, recheck leg load and running-specific exposure separately. | Can preserve aerobic opportunity but lose impact, terrain or technical specificity. | PARTIAL / LOW for equivalence / P |
| MOVE | Athlete is able to perform a priority session. Obstacle is logistical, and a feasible slot exists before the purpose expires. | Preview original and proposed slots, adjacent key workouts, race, locks, availability and lower-body work, including the next week. If no acceptable slot, offer a shortened option or omission. | Retains full session while shifting fatigue and displacing another opportunity. | DIRECT for scheduling / LOW for physiological superiority over skipping / P |
| RECOVERY | Original stimulus is relinquished. Athlete wants gentle movement, finds it comfortable, and has no reason to avoid it. | Offer brief optional easy activity or rest. Log actual load, remove the original quality target and reassess before the next demanding day. | May support preference and routine, but adds load and has no assured recovery advantage. | PARTIAL / LOW / P |
| REST | Athlete elects to stop, cannot tolerate even easy activity, or reports a concerning symptom. Original stimulus is abandoned. | Stop the workout or recommend no session. For concerning symptoms direct to appropriate assessment. Leave return unresolved until appropriate guidance and updated context. | Removes immediate exposure and loses today's stimulus. Fixed rest can unnecessarily restrict a later return. | DIRECT / HIGH for respecting stop or the consensus stop boundary, INSUFFICIENT for automated clearance / S |

## Contradictions and falsification by action

Each proposed comparison applies only to situations in which both choices are acceptable. No experiment should ask an athlete to override a stop signal. Define meaningful differences and measurement error before viewing results.

| Action | Rival explanation or null | Finding that would reject or narrow this candidate |
|---|---|---|
| KEEP | S3 allows a modest benefit from guided training despite pooled uncertainty. A noisy signal can coincide with genuine fatigue. | With otherwise comparable context, the flagged group repeatedly fails the intended effort under KEEP and an approved simpler edit improves outcomes without excess cancellations. |
| REDUCE_VOLUME | S2 supports volume reduction during taper only. Shortening a long run can remove the purpose entirely. | Under time pressure, moving the full run consistently preserves the declared purpose and next-session quality better. Restrict shortening to flexible-duration sessions. |
| REDUCE_INTENSITY | S4 does not distinguish excessive duration from excessive speed in a particular athlete. Slower running in prolonged heat can still be poorly tolerated. | After easing speed, internal effort remains excessive or the athlete fares worse than with shortening or stopping. Reject duration preservation in that context. |
| SHORTEN_INTERVALS | S1 found different oxygen responses despite matched work and similar RPE. Fragmentation may defeat sustained threshold rehearsal. | Short bouts repeatedly miss the predeclared sustained-exposure target or provoke faster surges, while fewer full bouts meet it at comparable later cost. |
| REDUCE_REPS | Removing late bouts may discard the intended late-session stimulus. S1 did not test early termination or chronic adaptation. | Shorter bouts or a move routinely retain more of the primary stimulus with equally good execution and next-session tolerance. |
| SUBSTITUTE | S5 pooled differences include zero but are imprecise. The substitute may load already fatigued muscles or be unfamiliar. | Replacement improves completion counts while running-specific execution worsens relative to moving or skipping, or the substitute is itself poorly tolerated. |
| MOVE | Runna permits skipping, and a successful move in a calendar says little about the rearranged week's quality. | The move violates any hard constraint, or adjacent key-session quality, athlete acceptance or recovery worsens versus omission in matched schedules. |
| RECOVERY | S6 gives little basis for requiring active recovery. An easy jog can still be work. | Compared with voluntary rest, later tolerance or athlete preference worsens, or there is no meaningful benefit to offset the burden. Drop the default preference for activity. |
| REST | S7 permits graded return in selected mild cases. S3 challenges blanket sensor-driven rest. | Clinician-adjudicated review finds the trigger repeatedly flags uncomplicated cases, or misses cases requiring assessment. Revise the trigger. Respect for an athlete's decision to stop is unconditional. |

## Applying one change to the remaining week

1. Separate a future constraint, a mid-session decision and a retrospective record. Preserve the original plan, accepted revision and performed segments. Unknown upload status is unresolved. Ask once whether the session was done, missed, partly done or replaced and why. Prevent duplicate imports or manual entries from creating a second training exposure.
2. Record what was actually achieved by purpose. A warm-up followed by an interruption produces easy minutes and no completed quality bouts. Two completed threshold bouts plus an aborted third contribute actual work and strain. An interrupted file alone cannot tell whether the athlete stopped. Keep uncertain segments unknown.
3. Recheck priority, recovery opportunities, availability, locked sessions, discipline conflicts and recent actual exposure across the week boundary. Omitted minutes create no training debt. Multiple missed sessions or a prolonged interruption warrant a separate re-entry review. Session C must validate any re-entry dose. Pace drift, HRV or a poor night's sleep cannot by themselves resolve the reason or determine a percentage change. Sessions B and D supply candidate observations only.
4. Show every proposed change together, including which goal loses exposure. Declining retains the existing plan and its separate actual record. Reject stale proposals if later sessions, symptoms or constraints changed. Reassess after the next relevant observation. Do not lower future targets permanently after an isolated partial.
5. Example: Thursday threshold stopped after two controlled bouts for a family commitment. Saturday long run is locked and Friday has lower-body strength. Credit the two bouts, leave Friday and Saturday as planned if otherwise feasible, and offer no automatic Friday makeup. If instead the runner stopped because normal easy effort felt unusually hard and that persists, the same file supports a check-in before Saturday. Any suggested change to the locked long run needs explicit athlete review. A race next week or a completed hard ride changes this comparison again.

The no-debt default is a conservative product hypothesis. A missed race-specific session with ample free space is a counterexample to automatic omission. Compare optional rescheduling with omission on subsequent key-session quality and athlete-valued specificity. A lower error rate with reason-aware rescheduling would narrow the default. No universally validated spacing, minimum repetitions or recovery-hour cutoff was identified here.

## Experiment for Sessions F and G

**First comparison:** distinguish a time-limited intact session from effort-limited execution. Freeze the primary intent before the session. Ask independent coaches blinded to the candidate's recommendation to label acceptable choices, the feature each choice sacrifices and whether there is enough information to decide. Allow multiple acceptable labels and report disagreement. Compare B0, H1, unchanged plan, athlete choice and a reason-aware proposal in shadow mode. No live intervention at this stage.

Collect purpose-authorized planned and performed work/recovery splits, reason and timing of interruption, modality, sensor provenance, athlete-reported effort and symptoms, schedule locks, recent actual load, subsequent key-session completion at intended effort, and athlete acceptance or rejection. Include an unknown-reason arm. Exclude unlinked records from dose-effect estimation and retain their count. Historical records without rejected choices cannot identify counterfactual outcomes.

Separate schedule feasibility, coach-rated intent retention, next-session tolerance, repeated unnecessary edits and safety referrals. An accepted proposal or higher completion percentage alone is insufficient. Split by athlete and forward in time. Report action-specific denominators, uncertainty, abstention, and results by goal, experience, sex where available, and missing-device context. Recheck results without wearable inputs. Preregister meaningful margins with athletes and coaches after a feasibility pilot, before held-out evaluation. Insufficient cases or unreliable reason labels leave that action ungraded. Any lock violation, unapproved write, duplicated dose or unsafe encouragement fails the structural gate regardless of mean benefit.

Only after feasibility and independent review, compare two genuinely acceptable athlete-approved choices prospectively in low-risk contexts, with consent, balanced allocation where feasible and uncertainty intervals. Shadow agreement cannot establish benefit. Exclude medically concerning cases from intervention comparisons and audit referral handling separately. No production promotion or ranking against commercial coaching quality follows from this report.
